Hispanic student enrollment began to fall after Trump took office, ending years of growth

Author

Will Hammann

Published

June 1, 2026

#

After years of consistent growth of Hispanic student enrollment across Maryland, the rate slowed to a decline in the first school year of Donald Trump’s second term.

Just after Trump’s inauguration in 2025, his administration rescinded the Biden-era policy that directed U.S. immigration enforcement agents to refrain from apprehending people at or around “sensitive locations,” such as hospitals, places of worships and schools.

That change has been just one facet of the administration’s intensified immigration enforcement policies. Its operations have shifted focus from near the U.S. border to within the interior, clashed with judges after illegal deportations and apprehensions, oversaw a record number of deaths among ICE detainees and killed several bystanders and civilians such as Alex Pretti and Renee Good.

Immigrant advocacy groups have warned how the administration’s immigration enforcement has impacted Hispanic students, including the more than 200,000 who attend Maryland schools.

Enrollment among Hispanic students in Maryland fell about 0.6% in the 2020-21 school year near the end of Donald Trump’s first term in office. Enrollment grew every year Joe Biden was president, until the first school year of Trump’s current term when it came to a halt, falling again to the pandemic-era 0.6% decline.

In the last 365 days of Biden’s presidency, there were around 1300 arrests by immigration enforcement, according to data from the Deportation Data Project. In the first 365 days of Trump’s second term, that number nearly tripled to almost 4700.

This story’s audience would be anyone concerned with the impacts of that escalation on the state of Maryland and the education of its Hispanic residents, especially the parents of students and anyone whose immigration status is under scrutiny.

Existing Reporting

The Baltimore Banner reported on that same ICE arrest data when it was released this March.

In January, The Banner reported that the graduation rate of Maryland’s Hispanic students fell by 4.4% in 2025, and that state education officials voiced concerns about how Trump’s policies influenced that trend.

A number of studies from before Trump’s second term by Stanford University professors and researchers from institutions like the National Bureau of Economic Research and the Migration Policy Institute have suggested a correlation between drops in enrollment and more intrusive immigration enforcement, especially through 287(g) agreements.

Reports from across the country, like by MPR News in Minnesota, have described immigrant families’ fears of sending their children to school. Maryland Matters reported in February 2025 on social media posts by school faculty members in Baltimore County presenting information about students to ICE.

Visualization

The difference in Maryland’s Hispanic student enrollment during Biden’s term and since the start of Trump’s second term can be seen across all of its counties.

2022 2026

The more than 830,000 Hispanic residents of Maryland make up 13.3% of the state’s population, according to the 2025 U.S. Census. Nearly a quarter of those people are enrolled in Maryland schools, densely concentrated in Prince George’s and Montgomery counties but spread all over the state.

The impact on enrollment can also be seen in individual schools across the state.

The schools with the sharpest declines in Hispanic student enrollment were also largely within those counties.

Potential Sources

Immigrant or Hispanic advocacy groups could help me gain a better understanding of the way immigration enforcement policy impacts education for Hispanic residents. Using that knowledge, I would focus my reporting on school PTO and community members of the high schools with the hightest populations of Hispanic students, like High Point High in Beltsville.

From my personal reporting experience in Hyattsville, I know members of the Cesar Chavez Dual Immersion School’s PTO who have voiced concerns of ICE activity and its impact consistently over years. I can see what those people have to say about this issue.

I’ve also spoken to George Escobar at CASA who is a great orator and excellent source on immigrant advocacy. I can also reach politicians at various levels, including Congressman Glenn Ivey of District 4 and local leaders of Prince George’s and Montgomery Counties.

What’s Next

The ICE arrest data from the Deportation Data Project is messy and rows and challenging to tie down to specific locations and jurisdictions. Still, it could be feasible to analyze, aggregate and map the data to directly compare ICE activity to counties or schools where the most dramatic changes to Hispanic student enrollment occured. Trump’s term isn’t over, so any further escalations of immigration enforcement could entail additional changes to the state’s enrollment rate of Hispanic students.

Though 287(g) agreements in Maryland were banned in February, some have only recently been voided and enrollment data isn’t recent enough to see an impact. After some time, I can examine the difference in Hispanic enrollment in localities where there used to be 287(g) agreements.

Data on graduation rates is released a year after enrollment rate data, but it’s worth reporting once it comes out. Similar to its impact on enrollment, harsher immigration enforcement targeting sensitive areas and the stress and fear it induces all pose a challenge to Hispanic students’ academic performance.

Businesses to Watch

Perdue Farms is the fourth-largest poultry company in the U.S., and the leader of the $1.6 billion industry in the state of Maryland. The company is largely vertically integrated, contracting farmers who lease chickens from Perdue, operations mostly based in the Eastern Shore. Though the region has relatively small populations of Hispanic residents, the demographic makes up much of the sector’s labor, including transient and vulnerable residents, such as those who are undocumented or who have guest visas. Crop farming is also integral to the industry as locally produced corn, soybeans and winter wheat are used to supply chicken feed. 

In 2023, Perdue Farms agreed to pay $60.7 million to settle claims the company conspired to lower wages and suppress labor competition at its facilities, as reported by Bloomberg Law. Polluted runoff in the Chesapeake Bay mostly comes from the state’s agriculture industry, leading to environmental scrutiny of poultry farming, the industry’s largest sector. Perdue Farms is a private company, posing a barrier to transparency and reporting. 

Unlike Maryland’s agribusiness, the hospitality industry is led by a public giant. Marriott International, headquartered in Bethesda, sprawls throughout the state and across the world from the heart of Montgomery County.

Marriott’s website lists 112 hotels in the state. According to a 2018 article by OpenArc Support in the Prince George’s Sentinel, there are 18 hotels employing 2,000 people in Montgomery County, which contains more than a quarter of Maryland’s Hispanic population. In Maryland and the U.S., Hispanic workers are largely employed by the hospitality industry.

This Q1, Marriott’s largest source of revenue was from fee revenues, such as licensing fees charged to franchise owners, at nearly $1.4 billion. Their largest expense was operating costs and overheads for owned and leased properties at $377 million and the company came away with $648 million of net income.
